# Formal Research Questions and Hypotheses

## Study focus

This study examines how Twin Cities immigrant- and refugee-serving nonprofit organizations use emotional public relations across organizational roles, time, and communication platforms during November 1, 2025 through March 31, 2026.

The design is comparative, longitudinal, and cross-platform. It treats emotion as a strategic and relational component of organizational communication rather than as a simple lexical feature.

## Core theoretical model

The working mechanism is:

**organizational role -> situational appraisal -> emotional mediation/framing -> platform-conditioned expression -> proposed public action or coping**

This model guides analysis after corpus construction. It does not determine inclusion in the dataset.

## Research Questions

### RQ1. Emotional prevalence and repertoire
How prevalent is emotional communication across the full nonprofit communication corpus, and which emotional repertoires are most common during the study period?

This question includes:
- whether emotion is explicit or implicit;
- which emotions appear;
- how intense emotional communication is;
- which emotional functions are performed.

### RQ2. Organizational-role variation
How does emotional communication vary across organizational roles and organizational configurations?

Relevant comparisons include:
- advocacy organizations;
- direct-service organizations;
- legal-service organizations;
- resettlement organizations;
- faith-based organizations;
- community-based organizations;
- economic-development organizations;
- broad multiservice organizations.

Because organizations are often hybrid, analyses should also examine the existing advocacy-service orientation as a continuum rather than relying only on mutually exclusive categories.

### RQ3. Temporal variation
How do communication volume, emotional prevalence, emotional intensity, dominant emotions, appraisal frames, and action orientations change over the study period?

### RQ4. Metro-surge change
Do emotional communication patterns change during periods of heightened immigration-enforcement activity associated with the Twin Cities metro-surge period compared with lower-intensity periods in the same study window?

The timing and boundaries of any surge period should be established independently through a documented external-event chronology rather than inferred from the communication corpus itself.

### RQ5. Platform variation
How does emotional communication differ across organizational websites and social-media platforms?

### RQ6. Within-organization platform variation
For organizations communicating on multiple platforms, how does the same organization's emotional communication differ between its website and its social-media channels?

This is the preferred test of platform effects because it reduces confounding between organizational mission and platform use.

### RQ7. Matched-event variation
When the same organization communicates about the same event or issue across multiple platforms, how are appraisal, emotional intensity, emotional function, and calls to action transformed across channels?

### RQ8. Emotion and action orientation
How are specific appraisals and emotions associated with proposed forms of public response, including collective action, legal-service seeking, service use, donation, volunteering, information seeking, reassurance, safety behavior, and community support?

### RQ9. Emotional mediation
How do nonprofit organizations function as emotional intermediaries between institutional crisis and affected publics, and how does this mediation vary by organizational role, temporal context, and platform?

## Confirmatory Hypotheses

The hypotheses below are intentionally limited to relationships that can be defended theoretically in advance. They should be tested alongside the broader exploratory research questions.

### H1. Organizational-role differences in emotional strategy
Emotional communication will vary systematically by organizational role and advocacy-service orientation.

This is an omnibus hypothesis: it predicts patterned differences but does not assume in advance that one organizational category is universally more emotional than another.

### H2. Advocacy orientation and mobilizing emotion
As organizations become more advocacy-oriented, their communications will be more likely to combine appraisals of injustice, blame, or institutional responsibility with mobilizing emotional functions and collective-action orientations.

Relevant emotions may include anger, outrage, solidarity, urgency, and hope, but the hypothesis concerns the appraisal-function-action configuration rather than any single emotion.

### H3. Service orientation and coping-oriented emotion
As organizations become more service-oriented, their communications will be more likely to combine appraisals of threat, vulnerability, or uncertainty with reassurance, care, warning, or coping-oriented communication and service-seeking or safety-related action orientations.

### H4. Heightened-period emotional intensity
Average emotional intensity and the proportion of communications containing emotional framing will increase during independently identified periods of heightened immigration-enforcement activity relative to lower-intensity periods within the study window.

### H5. Heightened-period communication volume
Organizations will publish a higher volume of communication during periods of heightened immigration-enforcement activity than during lower-intensity periods, controlling where possible for organization-level baseline communication frequency.

### H6. Platform-conditioned emotional expression
Within the same organizations, social-media communication will exhibit greater emotional immediacy and intensity than website communication.

This hypothesis should be tested at the item level with organization-level controls or fixed effects where feasible.

### H7. Website institutionalization
Within the same organizations, website communication will be more likely than social-media communication to contain formal informational, legal, service, resource, or institutional-authority functions.

This does not imply that websites are unemotional; rather, the expected difference is in dominant communicative function and presentation.

### H8. Social-media mobilization
Within the same organizations, social-media communication will be more likely than website communication to contain relational or mobilizing functions, including solidarity, urgency, attendance requests, sharing, volunteering, donating, or collective-action cues.

### H9. Appraisal-emotion-action alignment
Items that frame situations in terms of injustice or attributed responsibility will be more likely to contain anger, outrage, solidarity, or mobilizing action orientations than items framed primarily in terms of threat or uncertainty.

### H10. Threat-coping alignment
Items that frame situations in terms of threat, vulnerability, or uncertainty will be more likely to contain fear, anxiety, care, reassurance, warning, service-seeking, or safety/coping orientations than items framed primarily around injustice or responsibility.

## Exploratory propositions to avoid prematurely converting into hypotheses

The following should remain exploratory until the corpus and pilot coding are examined:

- whether advocacy organizations are more emotional overall than service organizations;
- whether one specific emotion dominates during the metro-surge period;
- whether Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, YouTube, or X differ from one another in consistent ways;
- whether faith-based organizations use distinctive emotional repertoires;
- whether legal organizations differ from resettlement organizations in emotional intensity;
- whether emotional intensity rises before, during, or after specific enforcement events;
- whether the same external event produces different emotional trajectories across organizational types.

These should be analyzed through RQs or post hoc exploratory analyses rather than treated as confirmatory predictions.

## Operational outcomes to construct after collection

Potential dependent variables include:

1. emotional presence (binary or multinomial);
2. emotional intensity (ordinal or continuous index after pilot validation);
3. number of distinct emotions per item;
4. dominant emotion;
5. explicit versus implicit emotion;
6. emotional function;
7. appraisal category;
8. action orientation;
9. communication volume per organization-week;
10. proportion of emotional items per organization-week;
11. platform-specific emotional intensity;
12. matched-event emotional divergence across platforms.

## Main independent and conditioning variables

Potential predictors include:

- primary organizational role;
- secondary organizational role;
- advocacy-service orientation;
- faith affiliation;
- organizational community scope;
- platform;
- week/date;
- heightened-enforcement period indicator;
- event proximity;
- content type;
- presence of client/community voice;
- presence of imagery/video;
- cross-post status.

## Preferred analysis sequence

1. Descriptive corpus profile.
2. Emotional prevalence and repertoire.
3. Weekly temporal visualization.
4. Organizational-role comparison.
5. Advocacy-service continuum analysis.
6. Heightened-period versus lower-intensity-period comparison.
7. Within-organization platform comparison.
8. Matched-event cross-platform analysis.
9. Appraisal -> emotion -> action-orientation association tests.
10. Qualitative close reading of strategically selected cases to explain mechanisms.

## Modeling considerations

Depending on the final sample size and missingness structure, analyses may use:

- chi-square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical comparisons;
- ordinal or multinomial regression for emotion/intensity outcomes;
- negative binomial or Poisson models for communication counts where appropriate;
- organization fixed effects for within-organization platform comparisons;
- multilevel models with communication items nested within organizations;
- organization-week panel models for temporal analyses;
- interrupted or segmented time-series approaches only if the external-event chronology supports a defensible intervention point;
- matched-pair or matched-event comparisons for cross-platform transformations.

No model should be selected solely because it produces a significant result. Model choice should follow the measurement level, distribution, nesting, archive completeness, and amount of usable data.

## Interpretation principle

The study should distinguish:

- **more emotional communication** from **different emotional strategy**;
- emotional intensity from emotional valence;
- organizational mission effects from platform effects;
- crisis-period change from ordinary baseline communication;
- explicit emotional language from implicit affective framing.

The strongest theoretical contribution will come from explaining configurations of appraisal, emotion, function, and action rather than simply ranking organizations by emotional word counts.

## Relationship to data collection

These RQs and hypotheses do not alter the census protocol. All publicly accessible communication within the study window remains eligible regardless of whether it is emotional, related to immigration enforcement, routine, cultural, celebratory, administrative, or service-oriented.

The purpose of fixing these questions now is to reduce retrospective hypothesis construction while protecting the independence of corpus collection from later theory testing.
