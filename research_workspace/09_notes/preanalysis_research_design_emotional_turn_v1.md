# Pre-analysis Research Design Memo: Emotional Turn in Nonprofit Public Relations

## Project purpose

This study examines how Twin Cities immigrant- and refugee-serving nonprofit organizations communicate emotion across organizational roles, time, and platforms during November 1, 2025 through March 31, 2026. The empirical goal is not merely to identify emotional language, but to explain how organizations appraise a changing public environment, mediate emotion, and propose forms of public action or coping.

This memo is prospective. It records the analytical logic before data collection is finalized so that later coding and interpretation can be separated from corpus construction.

## Core methodological commitment

The communication corpus will be collected as a census of every publicly accessible communication item from every organization in the sampling frame during the study period. Collection will not pre-filter by immigration enforcement, ICE, crisis relevance, emotion, political content, or presumed importance.

Website and social-media items will be collected before emotional coding. Routine service communication, cultural communication, fundraising, organizational updates, legal advocacy, crisis communication, events, resources, newsletters, announcements, videos, and other dated public communication will remain in the corpus.

Social-media collection should include as many active official organizational channels as can be defensibly verified, including Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, YouTube, X/Twitter, TikTok, and other public platforms where organizations maintain official accounts. Each platform exposure should remain a separate observation even when substantially similar content appears elsewhere.

Zero retrieved items will not be interpreted as organizational silence unless the relevant archive is demonstrably complete. Blocked, incomplete, undated, or unenumerable archives will be documented as retrieval limitations or partial archives.

## Substantive research questions

### RQ1: Emotional prevalence and repertoire
How frequently do nonprofit organizations use emotion in public communication during the study period, and which emotional repertoires appear most often?

Potential emotions include anger, outrage, fear, anxiety, grief, sadness, hope, solidarity, care, reassurance, pride, gratitude, urgency, compassion, and resilience. The final codebook should permit additional inductively observed categories.

### RQ2: Organizational variation
How does emotional communication vary by organizational role and organizational configuration?

Comparisons may include advocacy, legal services, resettlement, direct service, faith-based support, community-based organizations, economic development, and broad multiservice organizations. Because many organizations are hybrid, analyses should preserve both primary/secondary role classifications and a broader advocacy-service orientation rather than forcing every organization into a single rigid type.

### RQ3: Temporal change
Does the volume, intensity, or composition of emotional communication change over time, particularly during periods of heightened immigration enforcement and the Twin Cities metro-surge period?

Weekly analysis should examine:
- total communication volume;
- proportion of items containing explicit or implicit emotion;
- emotional intensity;
- dominant emotions;
- appraisal frames;
- emotional functions;
- action orientations.

Temporal patterns should be tested rather than assumed. Any identified spike should be demonstrated empirically and interpreted alongside dated external events only after the communication time series is constructed.

### RQ4: Cross-platform variation
Does the same organization communicate differently on its website and on social-media platforms?

The strongest comparison is within-organization and, where possible, within-event. This design reduces the risk of attributing organizational differences to platform effects.

Potential contrasts include whether websites privilege institutional authority, formal statements, legal information, resources, permanence, and reassurance, while social media privilege immediacy, relational communication, visual expression, solidarity, mobilization, and stronger emotional intensity. These are propositions to test, not assumptions.

### RQ5: Matched-message adaptation across channels
When an organization communicates the same underlying event, announcement, resource, campaign, crisis update, or call to action across its website and one or more social-media platforms, how does the message change across channels?

Matched-message analysis should examine changes in:
- headline or caption framing;
- emotional vocabulary and intensity;
- appraisal of threat, injustice, care, solidarity, or resilience;
- calls to action;
- immediacy and urgency;
- visual and audiovisual presentation;
- audience address and relational language;
- amount of contextual or legal information;
- personalization, testimonial, or community voice;
- temporal sequencing across channels.

A matched message is not assumed to be textually identical. It may be linked by a shared event, announcement, URL, image, video, campaign, named initiative, date proximity, or clearly corresponding substantive content. Confidence in each match should be documented rather than inferred casually.

### RQ6: Communication strategy and public response
How are emotional appraisals connected to strategic communication functions and proposed forms of public action or coping?

The analysis should examine whether different emotional repertoires are associated with different action orientations such as protest, contacting officials, donating, seeking legal assistance, attending events, staying informed, using services, supporting neighbors, or collective solidarity.

## Proposed theoretical mechanism

The working mechanism is:

**organizational role -> appraisal of the situation -> emotional mediation/framing -> platform-conditioned expression -> proposed public action or coping**

The study treats emotion not simply as rhetorical decoration or organizational tone, but as a mediating component of public relations through which organizations interpret situations for publics and orient possible responses.

## Working theoretical proposition

Organizations may function as emotional intermediaries between institutional crisis and affected publics. Their emotional communication may vary with organizational role, perceived responsibility, temporal proximity to crisis, audience relationship, and communication platform.

The larger theoretical contribution is therefore potentially a model of **platform-conditioned emotional mediation in nonprofit public relations**.

## Key analytical dimensions for the later codebook

Each discrete communication item should eventually be codeable on several separate dimensions so that “emotion” is not reduced to a single binary variable.

### 1. Situational appraisal
Examples:
- threat;
- vulnerability;
- uncertainty;
- injustice;
- blame/responsibility;
- loss;
- opportunity;
- resilience;
- community strength;
- institutional failure;
- safety/security.

### 2. Emotion
Examples:
- anger;
- outrage;
- fear;
- anxiety;
- grief;
- sadness;
- hope;
- solidarity;
- care;
- reassurance;
- compassion;
- pride;
- gratitude;
- urgency.

Coding should allow multiple emotions per item when warranted.

### 3. Emotional intensity
A reproducible ordinal measure should be developed after pilot coding, likely low/moderate/high or a comparable scale with explicit decision rules.

### 4. Emotional function
Examples:
- mobilize;
- warn;
- reassure;
- comfort;
- condemn;
- legitimize;
- build solidarity;
- encourage service seeking;
- sustain morale;
- express mourning;
- celebrate community.

### 5. Action orientation
Examples:
- protest/collective action;
- contact officials;
- donate;
- volunteer;
- attend an event;
- seek legal support;
- access services;
- share information;
- stay informed;
- support neighbors/community members;
- no explicit action request.

### 6. Explicit versus implicit emotion
The codebook should distinguish directly named emotions from affective framing conveyed through evaluative language, imagery, narrative, urgency, threat, or solidarity without explicit emotion words.

### 7. Source of emotion
Examples:
- organizational voice;
- client/community member;
- quoted public official;
- partner organization;
- testimonial;
- image/video subject;
- attributed external actor.

### 8. Organizational role
Use the existing sampling-frame classifications while preserving hybrid roles and the advocacy-service continuum.

### 9. Platform
At minimum:
- website;
- Facebook;
- Instagram;
- LinkedIn;
- YouTube;
- X/Twitter;
- TikTok;
- other active public platforms where included by protocol.

## Main comparison strategy

The analysis should proceed in layers:

1. Describe the full corpus and communication volume.
2. Measure emotional prevalence and repertoires.
3. Build weekly temporal series across the study period.
4. Compare organizations by role and advocacy-service orientation.
5. Compare platforms within the same organizations.
6. Conduct matched-message and matched-event comparisons where the same underlying communication appears across website and one or more social channels.
7. Compare adaptation within matched sets: wording, emotional intensity, visuals, urgency, calls to action, audience orientation, and timing.
8. Use qualitative textual interpretation to explain the mechanisms underlying quantitative patterns.

## Illustrative pattern to test, not assume

A possible pattern is:

- advocacy-oriented organizations: injustice/responsibility appraisal -> anger/outrage/solidarity -> collective action;
- service-oriented organizations: threat/vulnerability/uncertainty appraisal -> fear/anxiety/care/reassurance -> coping, safety, or service seeking.

This pattern must not guide collection or be treated as a coding default. The full corpus should be used to determine whether the pattern appears, fails to appear, or varies by organization, time, issue, and platform.

## Cross-platform analytical principle

Platform effects should be tested primarily through within-organization comparisons. Where possible, matched-message or matched-event analysis should compare the same organization discussing the same underlying communication across its website and one or more social platforms. This will help distinguish platform differences from differences in organizational mission or audience.

Cross-platform duplicates should not be discarded as ordinary duplicates. Each platform occurrence represents a distinct public exposure and should remain in the corpus. Related items should instead be linked through `crosspost_group_id` or a later `matched_message_id`. The analysis can then distinguish between (a) identical reposting, (b) lightly adapted cross-posting, and (c) substantial platform-specific reframing of the same underlying message.

For matched-message work, later processed data should preserve where possible:
- a shared match identifier;
- match confidence (high/medium/low or equivalent);
- source and destination platforms;
- whether the website item preceded, followed, or appeared on the same day as social posts;
- textual similarity;
- shared URLs, titles, images, videos, or campaign names;
- whether the social post links directly to the website item;
- whether emotional framing or action orientation changes across versions.

## Temporal analytical principle

The study period should be divided into weekly units after collection. Communication volume and emotional measures can then be aligned with a separately documented chronology of major local immigration-enforcement developments. External events should not be used to select communication items; they should enter only during analysis.

Potential methods may include descriptive time-series visualization, interrupted or segmented comparisons if defensible, organization-level fixed effects or multilevel models if sample structure supports them, and qualitative close reading of high-change weeks.

## Unit of analysis

The primary unit of analysis is the discrete public communication item. Each distinct dated webpage, article, press release, event page, newsletter, announcement, video, or social-media post is one observation unless the collection protocol specifies a documented exception.

Cross-posts should remain identifiable through crosspost_group_id or an equivalent reconciliation field so later analyses can decide whether to treat them as separate platform exposures or as duplicated content. Matched messages should likewise be linkable without collapsing platform-specific observations.

## Data-quality safeguards before analysis

Before emotional coding begins:
- finish the website census;
- finish the social-media census across all defensibly identifiable official channels;
- document archive completeness and retrieval limitations;
- reconcile raw -> interim -> processed data;
- deduplicate accidental duplicate captures while preserving deliberate cross-platform duplication;
- create and validate crosspost/matched-message identifiers;
- normalize organization IDs, dates, URLs, content types, and platform labels;
- preserve source text or sufficient archived content for reproducible coding where legally and technically feasible;
- create a dated external-event chronology separately from the communication corpus.

## Coding reliability

The emotional codebook should be piloted on a stratified sample spanning organizational roles, platforms, time periods, and content types. Definitions should be revised before full coding. Intercoder reliability should be calculated for major categorical variables and intensity measures if a second coder is available. Ambiguous cases should be retained and resolved using documented decision rules rather than inferred from organizational identity.

## Contribution to the Emotional Turn in Public Relations Theorizing

The intended contribution is not simply that nonprofits use emotional language. The study asks when, how, and for what strategic purposes organizations mediate emotion for publics, and whether that mediation changes across institutional roles, moments of heightened crisis, and platform environments.

The potential theoretical claim is that emotion in public relations is relational and situated: it emerges from the interaction of organizational role, situational appraisal, temporal context, audience relationship, and platform affordances. Organizations may therefore perform distinct forms of emotional mediation that orient publics toward mobilization, reassurance, care, coping, solidarity, or other forms of response.

Matched-message evidence can strengthen this claim by showing whether emotional mediation changes when the underlying substantive message is held relatively constant and only the communication channel changes.

## Separation of collection and analysis

This memo must not alter the ongoing collection rule. Data collection remains a full census of publicly accessible communication within the defined sampling frame and study period. Emotional relevance, enforcement relevance, and theoretical fit are not inclusion criteria. Theory enters after corpus construction, not before it.
